# Would a parent use this with a small child?

Read the parent's situation before judging. A good response saves the parent work and helps the child do, see, understand, or enjoy something appropriate to that request. More text, more activities, more citations, or more pictures do not automatically improve it.

## Primary decision

- **Use as-is:** the parent can use this now in the stated situation without inventing missing content or repairing the output.
- **Small edit needed:** the main idea works, but the parent must make a specific limited repair. Name it. This is not an as-is success.
- **Would not use:** it misses the need, leaves substantial work, bores/confuses through poor presentation, fails to deliver the requested thing, or creates a material factual or concrete safety problem.
- **Not reviewable:** the delivered output or required page is absent from the review bundle. Count it in the collection/completion report; never treat it as a quality pass.

Record one sentence explaining the decision and the main remaining parent task. If two versions are being compared, record preferred output A/B/tie/neither before discussing implementation details.

Distinguish a collection problem from a product failure: if the collector omitted a page that may have been delivered, use not-reviewable. If complete capture proves the product never delivered a required page, use would-not-use with missing-delivery. Neither counts as an as-is success.

## Six practical dimensions

For each applicable dimension use **0 = unusable**, **1 = substantial parent repair**, **2 = usable**, **3 = especially effective**. Use `null` and a reason when it truly does not apply. Do not combine these into a weighted safety/compliance score or require a perfect six-dimensional score before a parent may use an output.

| Dimension | What to inspect |
|---|---|
| **Right help for this moment** | Does it answer or deliver what was actually requested, at the requested size? Does it recognize a context update, a request for one idea, and a revision? |
| **Low parent work** | Can the parent begin without research, shopping, hidden preparation, making materials the page should provide, or translating a lesson plan into actions? Are waiting and cleanup honest? |
| **Fit for this child** | Can a nonreader participate when needed? Is the spoken explanation concrete? Are response spaces and instructions usable for the described age/ability and any sibling situation? |
| **Invitation to play or wonder** | When relevant, does it offer a real choice, challenge, discovery, story, or satisfying action? Does it preserve spontaneous play rather than replace it with a quiz or forced evidence collection? A plain direct answer can be excellent without a game. |
| **Useful visual or material** | When requested or essential, does the delivered page/image show the actual objects, relationships, setup, or actions? Can the child point, sort, predict, draw, or record with it? Inspect the page; decorative art and labeled empty boxes are insufficient by themselves. |
| **Coherent conversation** | Does it remember relevant context, preserve requested parts on revision, make a requested substantive change, and finish cleanly? Does it avoid redundant approvals, repeated setup, or restarting the lesson? |

A high score predicts usability; it is not evidence that the child was engaged or learned. Record actual parent/child observations separately when available and never manufacture them from generation or completion.

## Factuality and proportionate caution

Flag a **material factual error** if the answer or visual would teach something wrong, misrepresent an experiment, or claim an observation proves more than it can. Explain the incorrect claim and its consequence. Do not fail harmless wording differences or demand reference links for every stable everyday statement. Verify uncertain substantive claims through appropriate sources when judging them.

Flag a **concrete risk** when the proposed action creates an identifiable age-relevant hazard. State the action and risk. Ordinary supervised play is not a reason for a generic refusal or extensive warning list. Existing focused checks should catch these problems; this eval project does not require a new safety architecture.

Count **unnecessary refusal, irrelevant caution, repetitive approval, and safety-driven loss of the actual activity** as usefulness failures. The absence of dangerous instructions does not make an unusable answer successful. A short relevant precaution can improve an otherwise useful activity.

## Failure reasons

Choose at most two leading reasons so the next fix is clear:

- `missed_request`
- `parent_must_invent_activity`
- `hidden_setup_or_supplies`
- `too_much_reading_or_talking`
- `poor_fit_for_child`
- `play_replaced_by_busywork`
- `missing_or_unhelpful_visual`
- `unusable_response_space`
- `lost_context_or_revision`
- `unnecessary_caution_or_approval`
- `too_slow_for_moment`
- `material_factual_error`
- `concrete_risk`
- `missing_delivery`

## How to judge without overfitting

Accept multiple good ways to help. Case expectations describe the parent's desired experience, not exact phrases, an obligatory pedagogy method, a particular diagram type, a winner, or a fixed number of trials. Do not award a pass because the output repeats nouns from the question.

Read only what the parent would receive, with the relevant prior conversation. Evaluate actual preparation work from the steps and supplies, not the model's `parent_effort: low` field. Evaluate the final image/PDF rather than its spec or a claim that it is visual. Count a clear acknowledgment as correct for context capture, not for a request to make something.

Report the as-is count, small-edit count, would-not-use count, and incomplete collection count with denominators. Break out each parent situation and requested output type. Show recurring parent burdens before technical diagnostics. Calibration and a few human-reviewed examples come before choosing numerical promotion targets.


## Illustrated materials: additional visual-quality review

Inspect the final page at child-use size. For these explicitly illustrated requests, basic recognizable geometry alone does not establish a high-quality visual experience. Judge:

- Recognition: can a nonreader recognize the requested subject and its distinguishing details without the caption?
- Visual appeal: do the actual subjects feel warm, clear and inviting, with coherent art direction, rather than placeholder shapes or incidental corner decoration?
- Explanatory value: do pictures show the action, parts or relationship the parent requested? Labels alone cannot supply a missing visual.
- Composition: readable text overlays, useful picture size, clear white space and blank activity areas; avoid baked-in model text or answer leakage.
- Revision continuity: the same requested artwork is preserved for size/layout/text edits.

Record a separate evidence sentence for recognition, appeal, explanatory value and composition (and continuity when applicable). A beautiful image with the wrong content fails; a functional but crude placeholder can need an edit for an explicitly illustrated request. Judge visible output, not provider identity. Do not require a specific artistic medium. Model preference is not observed child enjoyment.

“No writing” means no required child writing, not no printed adult instruction, title or footer. A tiny game can be complete with a clear first move and child response; do not demand scoring, recording or extra rules. Pointing to different shares can be repeated without preserving a written record, unless recording was requested.


Judge the requested scope throughout the conversation: several ideas means several distinct usable options. A later why/what-if question needs an answer, not another menu or a regenerated worksheet. References to an earlier option must resolve correctly. For preschool and time-limited games, a brief imitation loop can be complete without explanation, scoring or follow-up questions. Phone-only tasks require actual recognizable pictures and no printing-dependent actions.
